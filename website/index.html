All 61 things SecuRAX does

Human-in-command tool approvals

When an AI agent asks to take a consequential action — move money, send a wire, email out — the gateway does not just block it: it holds the exact call for a human. `./securax approvals` lists what is waiting; `./securax approve <id>` grants a single-use, ten-minute approval scoped to that exact tool and arguments, and only then does the agent's next identical call go through. A different argument, a second attempt, or an expired grant is blocked again, and three blocked calls in a row stop the agent and ask for a person. Every grant is written to the tamper-evident audit chain. The dangerous tools (shell, delete, drop) stay denied outright.

Built and proven

Incident playbooks

Run an incident-response playbook as a live, ticked checklist. Pick a scenario (ransomware, data exfiltration, an exploited zero-day), start a run, and work the ordered steps through the NIST phases — triage, contain, eradicate, recover, report, learn — each step pointing at the estate's own command that does the work (the forensic kit, the regulator clock, the fix-by register, restore). It records who did what and when, will not let you close while a critical step is open, and ends by writing a blameless postmortem seeded with the run's timeline. A starting library for a BFSI SOC; add your own scenarios as data.

Crisis coordination

Coordinate a multi-agency cyber crisis and hit every agency deadline. When a regulator, a sectoral CERT or the police send a requisition — each with a short deadline and each item needing a specific record from a specific team — open it from a ready template, and every item is already assigned to its owning team. Track what is done, pending and overdue against the clock, generate the response pack (a register plus a filled cover letter) to hand over, and keep the ready documents for the moments that stall a real response: a forensic-tool policy-exception form, a chain-of-custody form, a forensic-collection SOP and a duty roster. The templates are generic data — add your own agencies, items and documents. It takes the tedious coordination off you: one command stands the whole crisis up, another names the single most urgent action, the estate auto-prepares every item it can source itself and chases the owning teams for the rest from one node (no duplicate nudges, no single point of failure), and ready crisis-communication drafts keep every channel on the same message. AI drafts; anything that leaves the building stays behind a human approval. Pairs with the regulator clock (incident) and the response checklist (playbook).

Built and proven

Crisis wargame

Rehearse the coordination of a cyber crisis as a timed tabletop, and score it. The hard part of a real crisis is not the attack but the coordination — the right action reaching the right team in time while deadlines collide, a key person is on leave and a second hit lands mid-response — and you cannot practise that from a document. A scenario is a timeline of injects (a thing that happens, owned by a team, with an expected action and an SLA); the clock starts, each inject fires on time, you respond in free text, and the estate scores whether the right action reached the right owner in time. It then writes an after-action report: the per-inject scores, the coordination gaps (what went unanswered, what was late, what was incomplete), and a specific, owned improvement action for each — which you can turn into standing lessons in one line. Worst-cases are built in by design: colliding agency deadlines, the one person who knows the system unreachable, a node that holds the clock going down. Scoring is deterministic (a keyword rubric against each inject's expected action plus timeliness against its SLA), so it runs the same offline and in CI; when a gateway is configured it also asks a model to grade the free text and to play the inject's sender under pressure, inheriting the gateway's rails and audit trail. The scenarios are generic data — add your own. Pairs with the real coordination (crisis) and the technical tabletop (continuity).

Threat hunting & post-crisis DFIR

After a cyber crisis the question is where the foothold is, how far it spread, and what was left to get back in. This drives Velociraptor — the Apache-2.0 endpoint engine — to collect and hunt, in two modes from the same packs: a single self-contained OFFLINE COLLECTOR with no server, the minimal mode that fits a DevSecOps pipeline or one suspect host; and a fleet of server + clients at mass scale for many teams. It ships NO proprietary forensic binary: the manifest declares each tool (Velociraptor, System Informer, and — gated behind a recorded EULA acceptance — KAPE, FTK Imager, Autoruns, Resource Hacker) with its licence, whether it may be redistributed, its official source and a pinned hash, and fetches it on use or from your internal mirror; KAPE-style triage uses Velociraptor's open Windows.KapeFiles.Targets, so there is no Kroll licensing exposure. A hunt pack (data, one per scenario — ransomware aftermath, payment-fraud persistence, lateral-movement / data-breach sweep, service outage, insider misuse, supply-chain compromise, quick triage) names the artifacts to collect, deterministic rules that score the collected output into prioritised findings, and turns those findings into an ordered, approve-gated remediation plan (only the actions whose findings fired, most-supported first; an AI agent drafts the narrative order through the gateway when one is up), which hunt report captures to feed the crisis pack and the lessons. The estate auto-prepares the matching hunt when a crisis is open (from one node, no duplicate nudges, no single point of failure), an AI agent drafts a second-opinion triage through the governed gateway when one is up, and every action that TOUCHES an endpoint — isolate a host, capture memory, rotate credentials, remove persistence — stays behind ./securax approve: a single-use, time-boxed, audited human yes. Pairs with crisis (coordination), incident (the clock) and respond (the evidence kit).

Growth map

The estate is built to start as an amoeba — the simplest living thing that helps today, one person, no server, offline — and carry the DNA to become an enterprise organism, with the dormant rail already built into each capability so it can grow when conditions call for it. This is where you see and steer that growth. It reads the estate's own conditions locally (how many nodes are live, whether a model gateway, a fleet server, an internal tool mirror or more than one team is present, and stress signals like load or a lost node) and, for each capability, shows the stage it is in, whether conditions now favour the next stage, and the exact rail to pull — ransomware hunting that is an offline collector today and a governed fleet when there are endpoints and teams; a crisis that is one responder today and per-team owners with SLA timers when there are teams and nodes; a gateway that is a local model today and a metered multi-tenant service when the organisation scales. The safe, in-box steps the estate can grow by itself; anything that provisions infrastructure, costs money or touches endpoints is proposed for your approval, never done silently. Nature is the architect: the organism grows only as conditions warrant, and falls back to the amoeba form under stress so no scaled-up part is ever a single point of failure. The growth map is data (governance/evolution.json); fitness evolution-declared makes every enterprise-capable feature ship its rail.

How is it deployed?

On your own hardware, as containers, in an afternoon. Your applications point to one OpenAI-compatible endpoint. Nothing is published to the internet.

Does it replace our DLP?

No. It adds the AI-specific layer DLP does not see: prompt attacks, model routing and an audit trail of every AI request and answer.

Any provider with an OpenAI-compatible API, plus local models on your own servers. You decide which data classes each model may see.
